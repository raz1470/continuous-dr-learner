Continuous Treatments Need Curves, Not Coefficients

Where double machine learning's partially linear model falls short for continuous treatments, a doubly robust and partially pooled alternative, and how far to trust its intervals.

Ryan O'Sullivan · Code on GitHub

1 · The usual approach 2 · Shape 3 · Robustness 4 · Many curves 5 · In practice Limits Questions Conclusion

A lot of the treatments we care about are not on or off. A discount can be 5% or 40%. A marketing team can spend £10k or £200k in a region. A user can get one notification a week or fifteen. The question is rarely "did it work?" It is "how much is the right amount?", and that question is about the shape of a curve.

When the treatment is continuous and chosen rather than randomised, most analysts reach for double machine learning, usually the partially linear model in DoubleML. It is a good default for a binary treatment. For a continuous one it has three limitations that matter in practice:

  • It returns one number. The effect is assumed linear, so a rise-then-fall or a saturating curve comes back as a single slope.
  • Its default score is not doubly robust. With a wrong treatment model the estimate is biased, however good the outcome model is. The alternative score is doubly robust, but only for that single number.
  • It gives one answer per population. If you need a curve for each of 200 stores, you loop, and the small stores get noise.

This article runs one simulation per limitation, plus a fourth on uncertainty, and shows an estimator that addresses all three: the doubly robust dose-response estimator of Kennedy et al. (2017), with a second stage that partially pools curves across a hierarchy.

1. The partially linear model, and what replaces it

DoubleML's DoubleMLPLR fits

Y = θD + g(X) + ε

where D is the treatment, X the confounders and g(X) is learned with any machine learning model. Models like g, which we need but do not care about for their own sake, are called nuisance models. Cross-fitting (each row's predictions come from models trained on other rows) and an orthogonal score (an estimating equation that is insensitive to small errors in the nuisance models) remove the regularisation bias from g, so θ comes with a valid confidence interval. The cost is the θD term: the effect of every extra unit of treatment is the same, wherever you start.

When the truth is a curve f(D), θ still converges to something. If the treatment is roughly normal given X, it is a weighted average of the curve's slope (a consequence of Stein's lemma):

θ = E[ Var(D | X) · f′(D) ] / E[ Var(D | X) ]

The weights come from where the treatment happens to vary in your data. Change who gets how much treatment and θ changes, even if the causal curve does not. Section 2 shows this happening.

The replacement estimates the whole curve, θ(a) = E[Y(a)], the average outcome if everyone received dose a. It runs in three steps:

Step 1
Cross-fit two nuisance models

An outcome model μ(x, a) = E[Y | X, A], and a generalised propensity score π(a | x): the density of the dose given X.

Step 2
Build a pseudo-outcome

Combine them into one number per row, ξ, whose average at each dose is the causal curve.

Step 3
Smooth, with pooling

Regress ξ on the dose with penalised splines, sharing strength across a hierarchy of groups.

The pseudo-outcome is

ξ = (Y − μ(X, A)) · ϖ(A) / π(A | X) + m(A)

where m(a) is the outcome model averaged over everyone's X at dose a, and ϖ(a) is the marginal density of the dose. The first term is an inverse-propensity weighted residual and the second a regression prediction. If the outcome model is right, the residuals average to zero and m(a) is the answer. If the propensity model is right, the weighted residual corrects whatever m(a) got wrong. That is what doubly robust means: either model can be wrong, as long as one of them is right. The ratio ϖ(A)/π(A | X) acts as a weight; section 3 shows it is better learned directly than built from a density when there are many confounders.

The binary-treatment AIPW estimator in DoubleML's DoubleMLIRM works the same way. The difference is that π is a density rather than a probability, and the final step is a curve rather than a mean.

Simulation 1

2. Shape: one slope cannot describe a curve

Nonlinear dose-response is the norm in practice, not the exception. Two shapes cover most of what you will meet:

  • Saturating. Diminishing returns. The first £10k of ad spend in a region buys more conversions than the next £10k, and the hundredth sales call to the same account adds almost nothing. Marketing mix models build this in with saturation curves for a reason.
  • Inverted U. Too much of a good thing. Deeper discounts win orders but give away margin, more notifications drive opens until users switch them off, and a higher price raises revenue per unit until volume falls away.

Either way, the decision you need to make (how much, and where to stop) lives in the curvature. A linear model removes the curvature by assumption.

The simulation draws 4,000 units with five covariates. Three of them drive the dose through nonlinear functions, a fourth changes how much the dose varies between units, and the first three also drive the outcome, so they confound the effect. The true effect is an inverted U in one dataset and a saturating curve in the other. Both methods use the same gradient boosting models for every nuisance function.

DoubleML's θ is not wrong: 1.45 is in the neighbourhood of the plain average slope of the inverted U across these units (1.54). It is not equal to it, because θ weights each unit by how much its dose varies. It is the right answer to a question you probably did not mean to ask. It says nothing about where the curve turns over, and in the saturating case it gives every unit the same marginal return, when units at high doses get almost none.

θ depends on the treatment policy

The second problem is subtler. The formula in section 1 says θ averages the slope over wherever doses happen to fall. To check, the inverted-U data was regenerated three times with the same causal curve, moving the whole dose distribution down by 0.5, leaving it alone, and moving it up by 0.5.

Three tight, non-overlapping intervals for the same causal effect. A team that cut discounts last year and a team that raised them would report different "effects of discount", and both would be right about their own data. A curve does not have this problem: θ(a) is defined at each dose and does not depend on how doses were handed out, only on there being enough of them nearby.

With a continuous treatment, θ describes your treatment policy as much as the effect.

Simulation 2

3. Robustness: one of two models will be wrong

Every observational estimate leans on models we know are approximations, and for continuous treatments the treatment side is the hard one. The generalised propensity score is a full conditional density, not a probability. In practice the dose depends on confounders through business rules nobody wrote down: discount depth set by a pricing team's tiers, budget split by last year's revenue, notification cadence set by an engagement score with a cap. The variance changes too: some segments get a narrow band of doses, others a wide one. A model of the mean with a constant normal error, the usual default, is easy to get wrong. So is the outcome model, especially when the response depends on interactions you have not thought of.

So the useful question is not "are my models right?" but "what happens when one of them is wrong?"

DoubleML's partially linear model

DoubleMLPLR offers two scores. The default, partialling out, regresses outcome residuals on treatment residuals. To isolate the robustness question from the shape question, this part uses a linear true effect (θ = 1), so the partially linear model is correctly specified. "Right" means a correctly specified parametric model; "wrong" means a linear regression on the raw confounders.

With the default score, a wrong treatment model biases θ by −0.08. That looks small, but the intervals contain the truth in 0% of replications, even though the outcome model is correct. The partialling-out score is only protected against errors in the outcome model. The IV-type score is doubly robust: bias 0.00 and 97% coverage in the same row. So DoubleML can give you double robustness if you change the score away from the default. The same point is made formally by Valentin et al. (2025), who show the partialling-out (Robinson) estimator depends on the treatment model, and propose an augmented estimator (ADML) that is doubly robust for θ. What none of these give you is a doubly robust curve: the robustness is for θ, the single slope from section 2. The last row, with boosted models for both, shows the same pattern in practice: 87% coverage with the default score against 93% with IV-type.

The curve

Now the true effect is the inverted U again. The dose still has plenty of random variation given X, so every kind of unit is seen at a range of doses (good overlap). Without that, inverse density weights become extreme and every weighting estimator, DR included, struggles. Each dataset gets a right and a wrong version of the outcome model and the GPS, combined four ways, and three estimators are fitted from the same pieces:

  • Plug-in: m(a) only, the outcome model averaged over X. Needs the outcome model right.
  • IPW: Y · ϖ(A)/π(A | X) only. Needs the GPS right.
  • Doubly robust: the pseudo-outcome ξ. Needs one of the two.

  • GPS wrong: IPW is off by 0.35. DR is off by less than 0.01, because the outcome model was already right.

Double robustness is about bias, not noise. When the outcome model is badly wrong the residuals are large, and multiplying them by inverse density weights makes the DR curve vary more between datasets (sd 0.07 against 0.03 when both are right). Kang and Schafer (2007) made the same point for binary treatments: when the weights are extreme, DR can do worse than a good regression.

The last row is what you would do in practice: boosted models for both. Here plug-in wins: error 0.03 against 0.09 for DR, with IPW far behind at 0.39. When both nuisance models are good but not perfect, DR adds the product of their errors back in, and an imperfect boosted density makes that product noticeable. But this simulation has only four confounders, and with four smooth confounders and 3,000 rows a boosted outcome model has almost no regularisation bias left to correct. That is not the situation double machine learning was built for.

With many confounders

Real problems rarely have four confounders. Customer-level analyses typically have dozens of features, most with small effects. A regularised outcome model then shrinks the dose's effect and lets correlated features absorb part of it, so the plug-in curve flattens. This is the regularisation bias that the DR correction exists to remove, provided the weights can be estimated.

Estimating the weights through a density is where it breaks. To test this, the Sim 2 data was extended with weak confounders (16 or 46 extra, correlated, each with a small effect on both dose and outcome; the total amount of confounding is held fixed). Four versions of the curve were compared:

  • Plug-in gets worse as confounders are added (0.13 → 0.36): regularisation bias, as expected. (Even at five, its bias is larger than in Sim 2, because these covariates are correlated with each other.)
  • Perfect weights would fix most of it (0.14 at 50 confounders).
  • Density-based weights make DR worse, not better (2.45). A boosted model misses part of the dose's mean when it is spread thinly over many weak features, and the density turns small errors in the mean into very large errors in the weights.
  • Weights learned without a density help (0.25). These come from automatic debiasing (Chernozhukov, Newey and Singh 2022), which learns the weight by minimising a loss rather than estimating a density.

So with many confounders, double robustness only pays off if the weights are estimated well, and density weights are not. In further tests, entropy balancing weights, which directly make the dose uncorrelated with each covariate (Tübbicke 2022), did better still: bias 0.08 at 50 confounders, with standard errors that matched the real spread. Their 95% bands still covered the truth only 72% of the time, because a small bias remains, so the bands need widening. The package includes these weights and a check that sets the width on your own data (see the repo's README). Simulations 1 to 4 use density weights, which work well with a handful of confounders.

Simulation 3

4. Many curves: borrow strength across the hierarchy

The decision is rarely one curve for the whole business. Pricing teams set discounts by category, marketing teams set budgets by country and channel, CRM teams set cadence by segment. The groups are nested (stores in regions, products in categories, campaigns in channels), and their sizes follow a long tail: a few big ones with plenty of data and many small ones with very little.

Two simple options both fail on that long tail. One curve for everyone (complete pooling) ignores real differences between groups. A separate curve per group (no pooling) treats a store with 12 observations as if it knew as much as one with 500. Looping DoubleML over categories has the same problem and is worse, because it also refits the nuisance models on each store's handful of rows.

Partial pooling sits between them. Each store's curve is the global curve, plus a regional deviation, plus a store deviation:

θstore(a) = B(a)′ ( βglobal + bregion + bstore )

B(a) is a set of spline basis functions. The deviations get a ridge penalty, which is the same as treating them as random effects. A small store's deviation is shrunk toward its region, and a region's toward the global curve. The penalty applies to each group's shape; its overall level is left almost free, so stores with very different baselines can still share a shape. A big store has enough data to overrule the shrinkage. How strongly to shrink at each level is chosen from the data by generalised cross-validation (GCV, a fast approximation to leave-one-out error), so you do not set it by hand.

Because the DR pseudo-outcome is just a number per row, this second stage can be any regression. The DR step handles confounding; the pooling step handles the hierarchy. For store-level curves, the averages inside m(a) and ϖ(a) are taken within each store.

The simulation has 8 regions with 25 stores each. Store sizes are log-normal, from 8 to several hundred observations, and each store's true curve is the inverted U plus a random regional and store deviation. Each store also has its own baseline, which raises both its doses and its outcomes, so there is store-level confounding to remove.

5. Using it

The companion repo wraps the three steps in one call. This runs as written, on the store data from section 4:

import numpy as np
from sklearn.ensemble import HistGradientBoostingRegressor as HGB
from drcurves import fit_dose_response
from drcurves.simulate import sim_hierarchical

df, _, _ = sim_hierarchical(seed=0)            # columns: region, store, x0..x2, A, Y

model = fit_dose_response(
    X=df[["x0", "x1", "x2"]], A=df["A"], Y=df["Y"],
    outcome_model=HGB(),
    groups=df[["region", "store"]],   # top -> bottom; df[["store"]] for flat groups; None for one curve
)                                     # group codes are added to X for the nuisance models
print(model.diagnostics())            # weights, fitted dose range, penalties, groups with constant dose

a_grid = np.linspace(*np.quantile(df["A"], [0.05, 0.95]), 50)
curve, se = model.curve(a_grid, ("r0", "r0_s03"))      # one store
region, _ = model.curve(a_grid, ("r0",))               # its region
new, _ = model.curve(a_grid, ("r0", "a_new_store"))    # unseen store: falls back to its region

If the dose takes only a few values (tiers, or counts such as notifications per week), the package detects it and models the propensity with a classifier over those values instead of a density, because a continuous density is not a valid weight for a discrete dose. Three checks are worth running every time, and diagnostics() reports the numbers for them:

  1. Overlap. Look at the weights ϖ(A)/π(A | X). A long right tail, or a small effective sample size share, means some doses are rare for some kinds of unit, and the curve there is an extrapolation. Report the curve over the doses you actually observe; the package warns if you ask for doses outside them. Weights are capped at the 99th percentile by default. The cap adds a little bias; without it, a handful of near-zero density estimates can swamp the curve. max_weight_quantile sets the trade-off.
  2. The weights. For a continuous dose the default learns the weights directly (automatic debiasing). If you switch to density weights with gps=GPS(), check that the dose model's residuals look roughly the same shape across units.
  3. Shrinkage. Read the chosen penalties. Very large ones mean the level adds little beyond its parent; very small ones mean the groups really are different. Groups with a constant dose have no curve of their own to estimate and simply inherit their parent's.

Uncertainty

A curve is only useful for a decision if you know how far to trust it. The package gives four kinds of interval, all from the same fitted model:

est, lo, hi, _ = model.band(a_grid, ("north", "store_17"))           # whole-curve band
lift, se = model.contrast(0.10, 0.20, ("north", "store_17"))         # dose 0.10 -> 0.20
gap, se = model.contrast(0.15, 0.15, ("north", "store_17"), ("north", "store_04"))  # same dose, two stores
best = model.optimal_dose(a_grid, ("north", "store_17"))             # dose, lo, hi, at_edge
  • Bands. A pointwise band covers each dose separately. A simultaneous band covers the whole curve at once, which is what you need before saying "the curve peaks between x and y". It is wider, by a critical value found from draws of the fitted curve.
  • Contrasts. The change from one dose to another, or the gap between two groups at the same dose. These are linear in the spline coefficients, so their standard errors are exact given the coefficient covariance.
  • Optimal dose. The argmax of each drawn curve gives an interval for where the peak is. at_edge reports how often the peak lands at the end of the observed doses, a warning that the true optimum may lie outside your data.

The whole-curve band is about 1.4 times as wide as the dose-by-dose one, and it is the one to use before saying anything about the shape. This example shows why: at the highest doses the true curve drifts just outside the dose-by-dose band, but it stays inside the whole-curve band throughout. The peak is pinned down to a range rather than a point (0.81 to 1.41, with the true peak at 0.93), which is usually the more honest answer to "what dose should we use?". Moving the dose from −0.69 to 0.62 changes the outcome by 2.13 ± 0.27 (truth 2.03).

The covariance matters more than the method. Penalised splines and shrinkage are biased on purpose, and a standard sandwich covariance treats the fitted curve as if it were unbiased. The default is instead the posterior covariance of the equivalent Bayesian model, σ²(X′X + P)⁻¹ with P the penalty, which is what mgcv uses for its intervals. Its intervals widen where the penalty pulls the curve. Marra and Wood (2012) show this gives close to nominal coverage averaged across the curve, rather than at every point.

To check the intervals, the Sim 1 and Sim 3 data were regenerated many times and each interval was scored against the truth. The bands are for each curve's shape (the curve minus its average), since group baselines are not the target:

  • One curve: the default pointwise band is close to nominal (95%), and the simultaneous band falls a little short (88%). Contrasts cover 100%, so their intervals are, if anything, too wide. Optimal-dose intervals cover 98%.
  • Many curves: the plain sandwich is badly overconfident (64% pointwise), because it ignores shrinkage. The default does much better (88% pointwise, 95% for the optimal dose) but is still below 95% for bands. Coverage is 90% pointwise in stores under 25 observations and 84% above 150. A likely reason for any shortfall in big stores is a small bias from the nuisance models, which stays while the band narrows; the simulation does not isolate it.

In practice, use the simultaneous band for any statement about the shape, ask for level=0.99 when a decision rests on it, and treat a 95% band over many shrunk curves as closer to an 85–90% band.

None of these intervals include the uncertainty from fitting the outcome model and the GPS. For a number that will drive a decision, run bootstrap_curves once. It refits the whole pipeline on resampled data (within each store, so every store's curve stays defined). It is slow, so use it as a check rather than routinely.

When not to use this

Every method here rests on the same assumptions, and some data breaks them:

  • Unmeasured confounding. X must explain how doses were assigned. Double robustness protects against a wrong model, not a missing variable.
  • Poor overlap. If some kinds of unit only ever get low doses, nothing in the data says what high doses would do for them. Check the weights, and restrict the dose range you report.
  • Many confounders. With dozens of features, density weights break and the intervals in section 5 are too narrow. Use balancing weights and widen the bands using the package's coverage check on your own data.
  • Doses set at the group level. If every customer in a store gets the store's discount, the store is the unit of analysis. Aggregate to stores first; row-level pooling across customers overstates what you know.
  • A spike at zero. Zero spend next to a continuous range of positive spend is a mixture the density model cannot represent. The package warns; analyse "any versus none" separately from "how much".
  • Crossed groupings. Store × channel, where channels are not nested in stores, is not supported. Each level must sit inside the one above.
  • A population curve from a hierarchical fit. The top-level curve of a pooled fit is a "typical group" curve, not the average over all units. Fit with groups=None for that.

Frequently asked questions

Why not bin the treatment and use a discrete estimator?

DoubleML's DoubleMLAPOS estimates average potential outcomes for discrete treatment levels, so binning the dose is an option. It throws away the ordering of the doses, each bin needs its own propensity model and enough data, and the answer depends on where you put the cut points. Within a bin, units with different doses are treated as if they got the same one, which brings back a version of the policy dependence from section 2.

What about a polynomial in the treatment, as in EconML?

EconML's DML estimators accept a treatment featurizer, so you can fit θ₁D + θ₂D². That handles a simple curve, but you choose the shape in advance, it is still a partially linear model with the same score, and it does not pool across groups.

Is there a kernel version of double machine learning for this?

Yes. Colangelo and Lee estimate the curve one dose at a time with a kernel-localised doubly robust score, and Klosin (2021) does it without estimating the density. Both are good options for a single population curve with careful inference. The pseudo-outcome approach here was chosen because the second stage can be any regression, which is what makes the hierarchical pooling simple.

Could I do the pooling in a Bayesian model instead?

Yes. The ridge penalties are Gaussian priors on the deviations, tuned by cross-validation rather than given hyperpriors. Fitting the same spline structure in PyMC or brms on the pseudo-outcomes gives full posteriors for each store's curve, at more computational cost.

Conclusion

The partially linear model is a good tool used for the wrong question. With a continuous treatment, the question is about a curve, and the three simulations show where a single coefficient falls short:

  • Shape. θ is an average slope weighted by where the doses fall, so it moves with the treatment policy. Estimate θ(a) instead.
  • Robustness. The default partialling-out score breaks when the treatment model is wrong; the IV-type score and ADML fix that, but only for θ. The DR pseudo-outcome survives either model being wrong for the whole curve. With few confounders a good outcome model alone can match it; with many, DR pays off only if the weights are estimated well, which rules out density weights.
  • Many curves. Looping over groups gives noise for the small ones. Partial pooling gives every group a curve, shrinks the small ones toward their parent, and lets the big ones speak for themselves.

The simulations use one data-generating process per question, known confounders and moderate sample sizes. They do not show how often each problem occurs in your data. The questions to ask are the same, though: is the curve likely to bend, which nuisance model are you least sure of, and how many groups need their own answer?

The estimator, simulations and the script that builds every chart and number on this page are in the companion repo.
